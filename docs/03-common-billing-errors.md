# Common Billing Errors and How to Spot Them

## Why this chapter exists

Billing is done by humans and software, at high volume, using codes that all
look alike. Errors are common — industry reviews routinely flag a meaningful
share of medical bills with mistakes. The good news: most errors are boring,
specific, and fixable with one phone call or one letter. You are not being
difficult by asking; you are doing quality control.

**Reminder:** this is admin help, not medical or legal advice. You're checking
that the *paperwork* matches the *visit*, nothing more.

## The Big Seven things to check

Go down this list once per bill or EOB. Most checks take under two minutes.

### 1. Wrong person or wrong coverage

- Your name spelled right? Address current?
- Is the claim on **your** insurance, or did it route to an old/ex-spouse/parent plan?
- Careful with family: one family member's care must never be billed under
  another member's policy.

### 2. Duplicate charges

- The same service (same date, same description, same code) listed twice.
- Sometimes it's a "charge" line plus it shows again on a second claim —
  check for the *same claim number* appearing twice with overlapping amounts.

### 3. Services you didn't receive

- Dates that don't match any visit (a "May 3" line when you went May 1).
- Descriptions of things that didn't happen that day.
- A **unit count** far higher than reality (e.g. "×11 units" of something
  that happened once).

### 4. Double billing for the same thing

- From different providers in the same system for one service.
- A facility fee *and* a separate-line version of the same service, when you
  were told one was included. Not always an error — but always worth one question.

### 5. Upcoding (a fancier code than the visit)

- A "comprehensive" or "complex" code on a routine, quick visit.
- Anesthesia or surgical codes on an office visit.
- You can't diagnose codes — and shouldn't try — but you *can* ask:
  "Can you walk me through what this code reflects about that visit?"

### 6. Out-of-network surprises

- An in-network facility where one *person* (an assisting clinician, a lab) is
  out-of-network and bills separately.
- If that happened at an in-network hospital, federal law often protects you
  from surprise balance-billing for certain emergency and facility services.
  You don't need to cite law on the phone — ask: "Was this person in my
  network? If not, does my plan treat this visit as protected?"

### 7. Numbers that don't reconcile

- Bill total ≠ sum of its lines.
- EOB "your responsibility" ≠ provider's bill (after the discount).
- Paid amount + your share ≠ allowed amount.

## The five-minute reconciliation habit

Once a claim arrives (or, better, once a week):

1. Open the bill and the matching EOB side by side.
2. Circle: billed, allowed, paid by plan, your responsibility (see
   [Chapter 1](01-bill-vs-eob.md) if those words are fuzzy).
3. Run one line of math: does *your responsibility* + *paid* = *allowed*?
4. Log the row in your [claims log worksheet](worksheets/claims-log-worksheet.md).
5. Only pay when the numbers match — or after you've asked (Chapter 4-style,
   cross-referenced from [Chapter 5](05-financial-assistance.md) if cost is the issue).

## Yellow flags (ask, don't assume)

- A service date you were elsewhere.
- Charges from a provider you've never heard of (often a lab or radiologist
  who assisted — usually legitimate, but confirm it was for your care).
- "Patient responsibility" larger than usual right after a plan change or job change —
  coverage effective dates slip a lot.
- A collection notice for a bill you have never seen.

## What a corrected bill looks like

After a good dispute round you should see one of:

- A **revised bill / corrected claim** with changed totals;
- An **EOB reprocessed** (same claim number, new numbers);
- A **zero-balance statement** saying you owe nothing.

Ask for the correction in writing whenever possible, and log it
([Chapter 7](07-claims-log.md)). If a correction never arrives in a reasonable
window (say, 30 days), escalate the same way you started: one polite written
request, referencing your earlier letter's date.
