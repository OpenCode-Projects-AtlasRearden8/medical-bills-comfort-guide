# Bill vs. EOB, in Plain Words

*Admin help only — this guide is never medical advice, and nothing here
changes what your care team does.*

## The two envelopes

Two envelopes can show up after a doctor's visit, and people mix them up all
the time. Here is the difference, with no jargon:

- **The bill** comes from the *provider* — the doctor's office, clinic, or
  hospital. It is a request for money. It says "please pay us."
- **The EOB** (Explanation of Benefits) comes from your *insurance company*.
  It is not a bill. It is a receipt-like summary that says "here is how we
  processed the claim." It often says, in fine print, **"This is not a bill."**

The EOB is the decoder ring. The bill is the invoice. The trick is that the
invoice should eventually match the decoder ring.

> **Rule of thumb:** *Don't pay the bill until you've seen the EOB — or confirmed
> with your insurer that no EOB is coming.* If you only have a bill and no
> EOB, it is perfectly normal to wait a bit (or call and ask) before paying.

## Reading an EOB, line by line

A simplified stand-in EOB, using pretend numbers:

| Field | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Provider** | Who sent the claim | Maple Family Clinic |
| **Claim number** | ID for this visit's paperwork | 2026-014822 |
| **Billed amount** | What the provider charged | $300.00 |
| **Allowed amount** | What your insurer says this service is worth | $150.00 |
| **Plan discount / adjustment** | The part the provider writes off | $150.00 |
| **Paid by plan** | What insurance covered | $100.00 |
| **Your responsibility** | Your share (copay, coinsurance, deductible) | $50.00 |

Walk down the math with round numbers: the clinic billed $300. Your insurer
says the fair price for that service is $150. The clinic agrees (it's in their
contract) and "adjusts off" the other $150 — they cannot bill you for it. Then
your share of that $150 is $50, and insurance pays $100.

## The "allowed amount" is your shield

Notice what did **not** happen above: you were never charged the $300. When a
provider is "in network," the difference between their sticker price and the
allowed amount is a write-off. This is why the EOB matters — it is the
document that shows the real, contracted price.

- **In network:** billed $300 → allowed $150 → you owe $50.
- **Out of network** (no contract): the discount may not apply at all, and the
  gap can be much wider. More on this in
  [Chapter 3: Common Billing Errors](03-common-billing-errors.md).

## What you owe vs. what they ask

By the time you pay, three numbers should agree:

1. Your share per the EOB ($50 in the example).
2. Your share per the bill.
3. Your own running math from [Chapter 2](02-deductible-and-out-of-pocket.md)
   or your [claims log worksheet](worksheets/claims-log-worksheet.md).

If they agree — great, pay it and file both papers. If they don't agree, do
nothing yet: flip to [Chapter 3](03-common-billing-errors.md) and
[Chapter 4](04-dispute-letters.md). Disagreement is *normal* and *fixable*.

## Phantom words, decoded

- **Copay** — a flat fee per visit ($50), set by your plan.
- **Coinsurance** — a *percentage* you pay of the allowed amount (e.g. 20% of $150 = $30).
- **Deductible** — the amount you pay each year before insurance starts sharing costs.
- **Out-of-pocket maximum** — the most your share can total in a year; after
  that, insurance pays 100% of covered services.
- **"Adjustment" / "plan discount"** — the part the provider agreed not to
  charge you. If you see it, that's good news, not a fee.
- **"Denied"** — the claim wasn't paid *by insurance*; it does not
  automatically mean you owe the full amount. Appeals exist (see Chapter 4).

> Reminder: this guide explains paperwork, not your plan's specific rules.
> Your plan documents and member services line are the source of truth for
> your real numbers.
